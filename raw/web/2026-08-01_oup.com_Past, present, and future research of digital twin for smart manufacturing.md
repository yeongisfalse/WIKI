## ABSTRACT

In the era of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, there is a growing focus on digital twin (DT) in order to advance toward smart manufacturing. Thus, researchers have conducted numerous studies on DT and extensively developed related technologies. There are many studies that apply and analyse DT to actual manufacturing sites for the realization of a smart factory, but it is necessary to clearly consider which part of DT is applied and what function it performs in manufacturing. As such, this study analysed and classified prior literature based on various phases of product lifecycle management, an application field of DT in manufacturing, and the hierarchy level axis of Reference Architecture Model Industry 4.0, the target scope of DT. Accordingly, this study identified research trends in the past and present as well as analysed and identified the major functions of DT (prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, improvement, and control). Through a gab study on the inadequate aspects of past and present researches, this study proposes directions for future studies on DT and a system architecture that can perform all the functions of DT.

## 1\. Introduction

As technologies develop in the Fourth Industrial Revolution, the highly competitive manufacturing industry is undergoing a paradigm shift toward smart manufacturing through data-based digitalization in order to improve production capability (Davis *et al*., 2012; Uhlemann *et al*., 2017; Papetti *et al*., 2020; Scafà *et al*., 2020). Advances in information technology, such as artificial intelligence (AI), big data, cloud computing, and the Internet of Things (IoT), have enabled the digitalization of the manufacturing industry (Tao & Qi, 2017; Qi & Tao, 2018). In addition to digitalization, digital twin (DT) has garnered considerable attention, with a focus on enhancing and developing the global manufacturing industry (Rosen *et al*., 2015; Lim *et al*., 2019).

DT creates digitized virtual models for simulating physical objects (Hochhalter *et al*., 2014). These virtual models can reflect the past and present states of the physical objects, analyse and predict dynamic changes, and transfer optimized values to the physical object (Rosen *et al*., 2015; Qi & Tao, 2018). Through this, DT can link the physical and cyber worlds to enable the optimization of the entire manufacturing process (Tao *et al*., 2018a). Ever since Michael Grieves first proposed the concept, researchers across numerous industrial fields such as manufacturing, smart cities, healthcare, construction, shopping, and oil and gas have conducted studies on applying DT (Grieves, 2014; Pires *et al*., 2019). DT can be applied in the manufacturing field for product design, manufacturing, and maintenance (Pires *et al*., 2019), enabling the monitoring of facilities and other resources, as well as optimized control and command (Kitain, 2018). Moreover, DT can control and monitor not only facilities but also processes, and it can be applied to maintenance prediction, decision-making support, product development, etc., to help improve productivity and efficiency. Thus, it is an essential technology for realizing smart factories (Chen *et al*., 2017; Pires *et al*., 2019). The original concept of DT was based on product lifecycle management (PLM) (Grieves, 2005a, b, 2011). PLM refers to the effective management of a company’s products from product planning to disposal. DT, which generates and efficiently utilizes integrated cyber and physical data, is necessary to achieve better management and control (Stark, 2015; Tao *et al*., 2018a). As a result, several studies applying DT to various phases of PLM are in progress. Additionally, among the several phases of PLM, many DT studies are being conducted on the inside, which is the manufacturing site of a factory, and the outside, which is the delivery stage after product production. The product lifecycle and value stream are combined and hierarchically structured to define the components of Industry 4.0. Of the three axes of Reference Architecture Model Industry 4.0 (RAMI 4.0), which provides a flexible explanation of the Industry 4.0 environment, researchers are actively investigating the hierarchy level axis (Adolphs *et al*., 2015). There has been abundant research on the application of DT to each phase of PLM and parts corresponding to the hierarchy level axis of RAMI 4.0 in the manufacturing field.

This study investigates the scope of prior literature, the functions of DT (i.e. the reason for its application) and its contributions. It also identifies and explains the practical application of DT in industrial fields. By analysing applications of DT in the past and present, future research directions for its application in manufacturing are proposed. The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section introduces the concept emergence and definitions of DT as well as PLM and RAMI 4.0. Section explains the purpose of this study and the literature search method, performs a literature search, and then selects and analyses the papers used in this study. Through this, the application scope of prior literature and commonalities of DT functions are described. Section explains the functions and cases of DT classified in selected paper and introduces representative case studies. Section proposes future directions for DT research through an analysis of past and present studies applying DT. Finally, Section summarizes and concludes the study.

## 2\. DT and Its Application Fields

### 2.1 Concept and definition of a DT

The concept of using a “twin” originated from the construction of two identical space vehicles in NASA’s Apollo program. The vehicle that remained on the Earth was configured to mirror the state of the space vehicle during the mission and called the “twin.” Models of space vehicles that remain on the Earth during flight missions have been used to simulate alternatives, reflecting data on flight conditions to support astronauts in critical situations (Boschert & Rosen, 2016). This application demonstrates the potential of virtual simulation in linking physical and virtual spaces. A virtual simulation model reflects the constraints of physical assets without errors and direct training on physical assets to extract appropriate solutions through virtual simulation. As such, it is an efficient tool for extracting powerful actions for physical assets, and it is more advantageous than performing trial-and-error method on physical assets in terms of time and cost because it is possible to solve problems with physical assets in advance through experiments (Swisher *et al*., 2004; Barlas & Heavey, 2016). Nevertheless, because virtual simulation does not involve linking data between the physical and cyber worlds, it cannot actually generate a “twin.” Therefore, virtual simulation cannot be regarded as DT (Barricelli *et al*., 2019).

As mentioned earlier, the concept of DT was first introduced by Michael Grieves in an executive course on PLM at the University of Michigan, USA (Grieves, 2014). According to Grieves (Grieves, 2015; Grieves & Vickers, 2017), a DT model comprises the following three basic elements:

1. Physical space containing physical objects.
2. Cyber space containing cyber objects.
3. Link for data and information to flow from physical space to cyber space in order to synchronize the physical and cyber systems by exchanging data.

Although digital representations were new, but the technology at the time was lacking, so there was not enough information to reflect physical products as digital representations. The reason for this is that the information collected during the production of physical products was limited (Grieves, 2014).

In the decade following the advent of DT, cloud services, IoT, AI, big data, and other new-generation information technologies have greatly advanced, which have enabled the collection of information on the manufacturing site, i.e. the physical world. This has allowed interaction with the cyber world and the eventual realization of DT (Hochhalter *et al*., 2014; Wang *et al*., 2014; Tao & Qi, 2017). As a result of numerous studies on DT across diverse fields, it was named in Gartner’s Top 10 Strategic Technology Trends from 2017 to 2019, indicating its status as a vital technology (Panetta, 2017, 2018, 2019). As shown in Table 1, a DT has been defined in various ways by several researchers.

### 2.2 Application fields of DT

#### 2.2.1 Application fields in product lifecycle

Dean (1950) first presented the concept of product lifecycle in 1950. Product lifecycle covers all processes of a product—from market acceptance to product disposal—and it is divided into four stages: introduction, growth, maturity or stabilization, and decline (Rink & Swan, 1979). Amid the rise of concurrent engineering, product lifecycle has expanded into the engineering field and has been redefined to encompass the entire process, including product demand analysis, design, manufacturing, sales, after-sales service, and recycling (Nevins & Whitney, 1989; Ryan & Riggs, 1996). Accordingly, it comprises the entire process of (i) product concept generation, (ii) design, (iii) manufacturing, (iv) transportation, (v) sales, (vi) utilization, (vii) after-sales service, and (viii) recycle/disposal. Large quantities of data are generated in each step, as described in Table 2 (Hsu & Woon, 1998; Cao & Folan, 2012; Li *et al*., 2015; Tao *et al*., 2018a

Big data generated during the product lifecycle can be analysed to provide systematic guidelines for production activities (Li *et al*., 2015). However, according to Tao *et al*. (2018a), the following problems exist in the product data management and application of PLM:

1. Data generated during the product lifecycle can form information islands owing to mutually different purposes and tasks.
2. There are large amounts of redundant data in several phases of the product lifecycle.
3. There is little-to-no repetition and interaction between many activities and the analysis of big data generated in the product lifecycle, making comparison with actual manufacturing sites difficult.
4. For big data applications, it is preferable to analyse real product data rather than virtual model data.

DT is required to address these issues as it can run virtual experiments reflecting design verification and validation as well as product characteristics. As such, many studies on this topic are underway (Maropoulos & Ceglarek, 2010; Quintana *et al*., 2010; Tao *et al*., 2018a; Lee *et al*., 2021). As a DT is not simply a model of the physical product but a simulation model that uses linked operational data, it can be used throughout the product lifecycle (Adolphs *et al*., 2015).

Moreover, it is applicable not only to virtual representation but also to the prediction of expected product operation. DT can also make predictions about real products to provide performance information, optimize design, and improve manufacturing systems (Adolphs *et al*., 2015; Rosen *et al*., 2015).

#### 2.2.2 Application elements in RAMI 4.0

RAMI 4.0 was developed by BITKOM, VDMA, and ZVEI to provide a new architecture model that meets the needs of Industry 4.0. As shown in Fig. 1, a three-dimensional (3D) model was developed to present all the features of the interconnected technical characteristics (Zezulka *et al*., 2016; Uslar & Sebastian, 2018; Yli-Ojanperä *et al*., 2019). The product lifecycle and value stream are combined and hierarchically structured to define the components of Industry 4.0, and it provides a flexible explanation of the Industry 4.0 environment (Adolphs *et al*., 2015). Each axis of RAMI4.0 is summarized as follows (Adolphs *et al*., 2015; Hankel & Rexroth, 2015):

- Layers axis: Consists of six layers that explain an object decomposed into structured characteristics according to each layer such as asset, integration, communication, information, functional, and business. Thus, it is a virtual map of the object. Key aspects of Industry 4.0 can be mapped within the layers, lifecycle and value stream, and hierarchy level axes, and objects can be classified according to the models. RAMI 4.0 can be used to flexibly explain and implement Industry 4.0 concepts.
- Lifecycle and value stream axis: This represents the lifecycle by classifying facilities or products into types and instances based on IEC 62890. IEC 62890 specifies the basic principle about lifecycle management of systems and components used for industrial process measurement, control, and automation, which supports the lifetime of product type and instance.
- Hierarchy level axis: Contains hierarchical levels representing various functions within a facility or factory based on IEC 62264 and IEC 61512. IEC 62264 specifies the basic model for enterprise-control system integration and IEC 61512 provides specific model for batch control. These two standards support understanding of the manufacturing system as they provide descriptions of the resources within the manufacturing site that require production and operation management. To encompass as many fields as possible from manufacturing system, the hierarchy levels contain product, field devices, control devices, station, work unit, enterprise, and connected world.

There are many studies that have utilized some of the RAMI 4.0 components described above in DT, as well as numerous cases applying components to the inside or outside of plants of the hierarchy levels axis. The hierarchy levels axis is defined with terms to cover as many fields as possible (Adolphs *et al*., 2015). From a manufacturing perspective, generally used terms can be redefined as “product,” “machine,” “process,” “factory (plant),” and “logistics.” Similar to the detailed components of the hierarchy levels axis, these include not only the products to be manufactured and the production facilities but also the part for cooperation (Adolphs *et al*., 2015).

### 2.3 Technologies of smart manufacturing for DT application

The definition of smart manufacturing has been not established yet; according to the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), smart manufacturing has been defined as “a manufacturing system which is fully integrated and collaborative to satisfy dynamic demands and requirements of customer needs, factories, and supply chain network in real time” (NIST, 2015). Similarly, smart manufacturing has been defined as “a novel production pattern integrating manufacturing assets with sensors, computing platforms, information and communication technology (ICT), control, simulation, data-intensive modeling, and engineering” (Kusiak, 2018). It means that smart manufacturing is a revolution of the manufacturing industry to resolve numerous complicated and irregular situations. On the other hand, the ICT including cyber-physical systems (CPS), IoT, cloud computing, big data, and sensors is necessary for smart manufacturing (Kang *et al*., 2016). These technologies are intended to achieve smart manufacturing through the interaction and integration between the physical world and cyber world according to the requirements of the manufacturing industry (Han, 2020; Rauch & Vickery, 2020; Tao *et al*., 2021). Furthermore, these technologies have greatly advanced following the advent of DT, and DT that links manufacturing sites and cyber models has advanced at the same time as element technologies have advanced. In other words, the utility of DT has been increasing through ICT, and DT has been considered as a significant contributor to this revolution toward smart manufacturing (Semeraro *et al*., 2021). Accordingly, research is being conducted to define the role of DT based on the interaction relationship between industrial Internet and DT and enabling technologies making technology more feasible (Qi *et al*., 2019; Cheng *et al*., 2020a).

As manufacturing paradigm has been shifted through Industry 4.0, a cloud-based CPS is suggested for high level of processes and production systems (Mourtzis & Vlachou, 2018). Cloud technology helps digitalization of manufacturing and data flow of production systems. It means that cloud-based CPS can easily identify the status of the shop floor through the digitalization of real-time data of shop floor and offers various services through implemented modules in cloud environment. In other words, real-time sensing, interoperability, and adaptability functions can be more effectively implemented by utilizing cloud technology to build DT or CPS (Mourtzis *et al*., 2018; Tepjit *et al*., 2019). Cloud technology offers various services through digitalized resources and it has changed manufacturing paradigm from traditional to cloud manufacturing (Xu, 2012). While improving the level of smart manufacturing, cloud technology brings up a new concept of DT, which is a Digital Twin as a Service (DTaaS). According to Pallis (2010), cloud technology enables that everything is offered as a service, for instance, SaaS (Software as a Service), PaaS (Platform as a Service), and IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service). DT as a service extends to DTaaS, which interacts vertically with consumers through augmented interfaces implemented with technologies such as augmented/mixed/virtual reality (AR/MR/VR) for physical, digital, and cyberspace as a service (Aheleroff *et al*., 2021). Also, this interface technology enables the development of various applications that can improve the interaction between humans and machines (Liu *et al*., 2018; Ceruti *et al*., 2019; Zhu *et al*., 2019; Dhiman & Röcker, 2021). In other words, DT is also digitalized like other resources in the cloud environment and offers important services as one of the versatile modules. On the other hand, cloud technology results in diverse concerns such as privacy, integrity, and security since not only CPS and DT require the acquisition, processing, and exchange of big data but also the information systems are managed on an external server (Tao *et al*., 2020). To address these problems, cloud technology for DT has to use encryption and security standards and enhanced confidentiality and also researches on standards and communication protocols (Popović & Hocenski, 2010, Lu *et al*., 2020).

## 3\. Literature Survey

### 3.1 Literature search method

This study was conducted to answer the following research questions (RQ):

1. RQ1: To which parts is DT applied in the hierarchy level axis of RAMI 4.0 and the product lifecycle phases?
2. RQ2: What are the functions of DT, i.e. the reasons why DT was applied in the studies?
3. RQ3: Among the functions of DT, where are there inadequacies and why?
4. RQ4: What are future research directions to address inadequacies in this research field?

Based on the above four questions, a literature search was performed through the process of defining keywords, searching literature, and then completing the analysis (Fahimnia *et al*., 2015). This study designed search strings to investigate where DT was applied in the phases of product lifecycle and hierarchy level axis of RAMI 4.0. The literature search was conducted on the Scopus database, and search strings were limited to article titles, abstracts, and keywords. Furthermore, to ensure the quality and relevance of the documents, the search scope was narrowed as follows: Given that the concept of DT was introduced in 2003 (Grieves, 2014), the period was set from January 2003 to December 2020. The document type was set to “article,” the subject area to “engineering,” and the language to English.” Table 3 describes the criteria for the article search in detail.

Next, 2465 papers were identified by limiting the search period to “January 2003 to December 2020.” This was further narrowed down to 817 after limiting the document type to “article,” 615 after limiting the subject area to “engineering,” and 499 after limiting the language to “English.” Subsequently, a screening procedure was performed to determine the relevance of DT application in the phases of product lifecycle and hierarchy level axis of RAMI 4.0 by reading the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, through which 91 articles were identified. These 91 articles form the basis of this study. The criteria for papers excluded from the screening procedure are as follows:

1. Only content related to the concept of DT with no real application cases.
2. Methods that do not involve simulation, contrary to the definition of DT.
3. Methods to improve DT itself rather than apply DT.
4. Methods applying traditional simulation rather than DT.

## 4\. Functions of DT in Smart Manufacturing

### 4.1 Prototyping

#### 4.1.1 DT-based prototyping

Literature on the prototyping function of DT used products as the application target. The following relevant studies were identified:

Zheng *et al*. (2018) prototyped a product through DT to increase customer satisfaction for the product. This study visualized the product, a mask, in the cyber world through DT, and then 3D-printed facial features according to the customer’s attributes using a cloud service to produce the product. Additionally, after identifying the customer’s facial features using a 3D scanner, pressure and breathing data collected while the user wore the mask were utilized for simulation through DT. Through this process, the mask was prototyped to tailor it to the user. Shikata *et al*. (2019) used DT to prototype automobiles to quantify the correlation between the customer’s ride comfort and power consumption and the influence of power-related parts on vehicle performance. This study was motivated by the demand for electric automobiles in the automotive industry, in which automobile control software is growing in complexity and environmental regulations are tightening. This study constructed, tested, and verified a virtual vehicle environment to develop a system that automatically charges an electric automobile when parked. Accordingly, researchers confirmed that DT can be utilized to prototype automobiles, an expensive product, before development. To minimize bottlenecks in complex construction sites, Lim *et al*. (2020) prototyped tower cranes through DT to generate digital models of assets and environments in a dynamic environment, integrate with the simulation system, and support decision making. To quickly generate the DT, the researchers proposed a general DT architecture featuring a three-layer technology stack, based on which they proposed a DT system that can manage disruption in a complex environment by improving its context awareness. Furthermore, they applied this DT to the asset design of modular tower cranes performing transport activities in oil and gas facilities, and experimentally demonstrated that the system supports route planning by recommending the shortest and safest routes through a dynamic environment layout model and the disruption-management function of the operator.

As described above, DT has been used to prototype products in previous studies. DT-based product prototyping has the following advantages:

1. Virtually implements a customized prototype based on data collected as users use the product and pre-verify the product’s shape through the virtual prototype.
2. After virtually implementing the prototype, performs tests to determine if it works normally without any problems.
3. Obtains necessary but difficult-to-collect information through tests on the virtual prototype.
4. Pre-verifies through the virtual model whether there are any problems among the parts forming the product.

#### 4.1.2 Case study

Hwang and Noh (2018) applied DT to a test bed constructed to prototype new products of a Korean cosmetic company. Among the parts covered in this case study, the present study explains only the prototyping part. Because the new product is designed without considering the problems that arise during production at the manufacturing site, it is necessary to predict these problems in advance through a prototype of the new product and transfer it to the product designer. To solve this problem, Hwang and Noh (2018) first extracted data from a 3D computer-aided design (CAD) model of the new product and compared it with past design data. They then matched the input design data of the new product with the related data of the past product to predict the defect rate of the new product. This predicted defect rate was highlighted in the 3D CAD model to visualize the predicted defect locations. Once there were no expected defects after redesigning the product and beginning manufacturing, they applied DT to complete the design. Thus, DT was applied to represent the prototype of the new product virtually.

As shown in Fig. 6 (Hwang & Noh, 2018), the system architecture in the study was designed to enable product designers to virtually visualize the predicted defect location as well as the appearance of the new product. To visualize and calculate the probability of defects and the prototype of the new product through DT, it comprises a total of three components, which are described in detail as follows:

1. Manufacturing site: The actual manufacturing site for producing cosmetic containers. Transfers information among products, production facilities, and processes collected in the manufacturing site to the DT manager through radio-frequency identification.
2. DT manager: Implemented on the cloud. Stores information among products, production facilities, and processes transferred from the manufacturing site. In addition, it predicts and visualizes the defect rate of a new product by matching the design parameters and values of the new product input by the user with the related data of the past product. If there is a defect rate, it is visualized and the 3D CAD file of the new product is transferred to the DT application.
3. DT application: Visualizes the defect location from past data related to the new product transferred from the DT manager through the 3D CAD file of the new product.

### 4.2 Pilot

#### 4.2.1 DT-based pilot test

Past research that used DT to perform pilot tests on the production system was conducted on machines, processes, and plants, which includes the studies described below.

Erdős *et al*. (2020) sought to verify whether the reconfiguration of a robotic cell that works on a given product could be realized when the cell’s work type had changed. For this purpose, they applied DT and investigated whether robot arrival or collision occurs, in addition to whether the robotic cell could be reconfigured. The path of the robot can be improved by repeatedly driving the DT while reflecting the robot’s movements, path, and work order, along with rearranging the layout of the work station. The researchers found that to revise and confirm the cell reconfiguration, tool wear, and robot movements, it took approximately 5 days in an actual working cell through the standard approach, whereas it took only several hours to a day through DT. Liu *et al*. (2019a) proposed a DT-based methodology for advanced design to reduce costs and increase the efficiency of an automated flow-shop manufacturing system (AFMS). This study compared and verified the existing AFMS with the AFMS designed and managed through DT, and found that AFMS applying DT reduced inventory, including WIP, by 28.6% and reduced costs by 22.2%. Hence, this demonstrates that DT can be applied to substantially improve the efficiency of AFMS. Zhang *et al*. (2017) presented a DT-based model for the individualized design of a hollow glass production line, which has numerous limitations in terms of capacity, layout, and process path. Based on the idea of “iterative optimization between static design and dynamic execution,” this DT solved an optimization problem combining process constraints and the analysis of configuration variables in the production line design. As a virtual twinning technique based on distributed simulation, this study used simulation as a verification tool for the optimization solution. Applying this technology to a hollow glass processing company in Chengdu, China resulted in a roadway utilization rate of 69.5% (standard: less than 85%) and an outbound high-speed vehicle utilization rate of 71.3%. This shows that the production line performance can be significantly improved through DT-based design.

These prior studies demonstrate that DT can conduct pilot tests on machines, processes, and plants. DT-based pilot testing has the following advantages:

1. Pre-verifies whether changing the parts constituting the machine causes performance issues.
2. When the resources constituting the manufacturing site must be changed, compares the investment cost or performance to the current manufacturing site to calculate an optimized layout.

### 4.3 Monitoring

#### 4.3.1 DT-based monitoring

Past research that used DT to perform monitoring was conducted on machines, processes, and plants, which includes the studies described below.

Wang *et al*. (2020a) presented a DT framework that can monitor a machine’s status in real time by linking the machine to a system. This framework was applied to a die cutting machine for DT-based monitoring. The DT outputs the machine status, production time, OEE, and order schedule and displays it to the operator. Han *et al*. (2020) presented a DT-driven monitoring and traceability system for a continuous casting machine with parts requiring frequent maintenance. The proposed system was applied to steel-making enterprises. It obtains information on the real-time operation status and work history status related to the machine parts and delivers it to the operator through a 3D DT model. Applying the proposed system improved maintenance efficiency by 6%. Zhuang *et al*. (2018) presented a framework for DT-based smart production management and a control approach for complex product assembly sites. This study provided monitoring functionality for logistics, workers, machines, and workstations through a DT-based assembly shop-floor production management and control optimization service. By applying DT to the assembly site and comparing the planned progress with the actual progress, the assembly efficiency was improved and the production cost was reduced.

These studies demonstrate that DT can monitor machines, processes, and plants. DT-based monitoring provides the following advantages:

1. Synchronizes real-time data of a machine to monitor and verify its status.
2. Synchronizes data on the parts in a machine to monitor the status of the parts and predict lifecycle and maintenance.
3. Tracks and visualizes the product’s location in the process in real time to analyse the logistics flow.

#### 4.3.2 Case study

Kim *et al*. (2021) applied DT to monitor a rapid dyeing machine in a Korean dye-processing plant. In a rapid dyeing machine, product defects arise according to the temperature of the facility. Machine factors have the greatest influence on product quality, which leads to economic losses. However, the dye processing industry is labor intensive, as on-site operators manually manage the facilities and produce products. It is challenging for operators to quickly read the gauge that displays the machine’s temperature owing to its small size, thereby leading to product defects.

To address this, a DT was applied that can monitor the machine’s real-time temperature information collected at the site. As shown in Fig. 10 (Kim *et al*., 2021), this system architecture was designed to monitor the temperature status of the machine by reflecting real-time information collected at the actual site. It comprises a total of three components to monitor the facility’s status through DT, which are described in detail as follows:

1. Manufacturing site: Actual rapid dyeing machine for dye processing. Transmits the real-time status information of the actual rapid dyeing machine to the DT manager via IIoT.
2. DT manager: Stores the real-time status information of the actual rapid dyeing machine. The quality prediction module in the DT manager synchronizes the abstracted data of the rapid dyeing machine and performs oversampling. The oversampled data are then utilized to start training the machine learning model and generate the sample data. These generated sample data are tested, and product defect predictions are calculated. Along with the product defect predictions, to drive the DT, the data are converted to objects and transmitted to the DT application every minute.
3. DT application: The operation module in the DT application reflects the object information and product defect predictions received from the DT manager every minute in the simulation engine. Through this, the rapid dyeing machine’s temperature status and product defect predictions are displayed.

### 4.4 Improvement

#### 4.4.1 DT-based improvement

Past research that used DT for continuous improvement on problems in manufacturing sites was conducted on machines, processes, plants, and logistics, which includes the studies described below.

Tao *et al*. (2018b) introduced prognostics and health management (PHM) for stable operation of equipment that operates for long periods in harsh environments and requires high maintenance costs in the event of malfunction or performance degradation. The authors first established a five-dimensional DT for complex equipment and then proposed a new method using DT-driven PHM. The proposed five-dimensional architecture consists of a physical entity model, virtual equipment model, service model, DT data model, and connection model. Additionally, the proposed PHM method classifies faults into gradual and abrupt faults and presumes that they can be predicted through the gradual degradation of components. For gradual errors, the early warning function is extracted from past data through the training phase, and in the prediction phase, features are input from real-time data into the training model to predict the cause of the fault. The authors also conducted a case study applying this to the gear motor of a wind turbine and showed that the DT method clearly improved accuracy compared to the existing method. Zhou *et al*. (2020) proposed a general framework for knowledge-driven DT manufacturing cell (KDTMC) toward intelligent manufacturing, which can support autonomous manufacturing through intelligent recognition, simulation, understanding, prediction optimization, and control strategy. KDTMC is a knowledge-based autonomous manufacturing cell that can serve as the smallest implementation unit for intelligent manufacturing. Here, knowledge-based intelligent technology refers to the ability to appropriately use knowledge in a dynamic knowledge base according to real-time manufacturing conditions. The digital space can simulate and visualize manufacturing processes based on the recognized manufacturing data. The manufacturing process performance can also be predicted or optimized through decision-making processes and intelligent analysis implemented through a public knowledge base and knowledge-based intelligent technology. The authors constructed a test bed to perform a case study, which validated KDTMC’s ability to provide practical insights into the intelligent manufacturing paradigm for intelligent process planning, intelligent production scheduling, production process analysis, and dynamic regulation. Ding *et al*. (2019) defined a DT-based cyber-physical production system (CPPS) and proposed a framework reference model to build the interconnection and interoperability between the actual physical shop floor and the cyber shop floor. The proposed DT-CPPS enhances the transparency of the production system, provides data and model-based simulation functions for autonomous manufacturing, and includes modules for operating and controlling the production system. Thus, it provides a monitoring interface based on the production progress status and resource status acquired in real time, and it uses a DT simulation-based method to solve tasks such as task scheduling, task allocation of logistics resources, and optimization. Furthermore, finalized decisions are fed back to the physical shop floor for subsequent control and manufacturing task management. Thus, the presented reference model and DT-CPPS will provide the basis for shop floors to develop toward smart manufacturing.

These studies demonstrate that DT can be used to make continuous improvements in machines, processes, plants, and logistics. DT-based improvements provide the following advantages:

1. Continuously updates and predicts machine status to then support maintenance-related decision making.
2. Calculates plans for optimizing and improving process and plant performance to support decision making.
3. Calculates optimization methods for logistics inside and outside the plant and the resources required to support decision making.

#### 4.4.2 Case study

Son *et al*. (2021) applied DT-based improvement to automotive body production lines in Yeongcheon, South Korea, to make continuous improvements in predicting production feasibility according to the production plan. In the target manufacturing process, it is difficult to predict whether production according to the production plan is possible because of product defects and equipment failure. Therefore, a solution that can predict the feasibility of production plans is necessary.

As shown in Fig. 12 (Son *et al*., 2021), this system architecture was designed to reflect legacy system information and information collected at the actual site in the DT to predict and judge whether production is possible according to the production plan, thereby continuously improving the decision making of the production manager. It comprises four components, as described in detail below:

1. Manufacturing site: Actual production lines to produce the automotive bodies. Transmits plant and resource information of the actual manufacturing site to the manufacturing cloud service platform through IIoT, as well as product, process, plan, plant, and resource information to the legacy system.
2. Legacy system: Stores and visualizes various pieces of information about the manufacturing site and transfers the status information of the manufacturing site to the user. In addition, it transfers information for driving the DT.
3. Manufacturing cloud service platform: Stores the information received from the manufacturing site and legacy system. In addition, pre-processes and then delivers data for driving the DT and visualizes the results from driving the DT.
4. DT application: The operation module in the DT application reflects the data received from the manufacturing cloud service platform in the simulation engine and drives the DT. By driving the DT application, the predicted production volume that can determine whether production is feasible according to the production plan is calculated as the result value. The result value calculated by the DT application is transferred to the manufacturing cloud service platform.

### 4.5 Control

#### 4.5.1 DT-based control

Past research that used DT to control the behavior of resources in manufacturing sites was conducted on machines and plants, which includes the studies described below.

To improve punching speed, the key performance measure of the punching process, Zhao *et al*. (2019) proposed a technology in which a DT model of a micro punching system (MPS) recognizes the context of the punching process to form self-adapting control behaviors according to the physical environment. The DT defines performance metrics to support online optimization and provides context-aware adaptability to changes. The authors developed a demo prototype MPS machine tool and experimentally demonstrated that the proposed DT-driven CPS directly validates and controls the execution logic in the digital model of MPS, quickly finds the malfunction reason, and inspects the system to determine whether it can meet the quality control requirements. The experimental results demonstrated that the positioning accuracy of the machine tool, an important factor in mass production, improved from 4.0 to 2.0 m, and the punching speed from 20–25 to 20–65 dot/s. To obtain stable surface quality in five-axis machining, Zhao *et al*. (2020b) proposed a surface roughness stabilization method based on DT-driven machining parameter self-adaption. The proposed DT is materialized using static and dynamic data, and the roughness is predicted based on pigeon-inspired optimization and support vector machine (PIO–SVM), which is affected by the cutter posture and the cutting force on the surface. With the help of DT, the surface roughness can be predicted online based on real-time input data, and the parameters of the machine tool can be adjusted simultaneously based on the feedback information of the predictive model and the parameter adjustment strategy. The authors performed a case study on a commercial five-axis machine tool for workpieces used in the aerospace industry. According to their experiments, the roughness prediction error was 8.69%, and the surface roughness was substantially reduced from 0.4882 to 0.2995 $\mu$ m by fine-tuning the adjustable parameters. Wang *et al*. (2020b) proposed a DT that controls the manufacturing process through deep learning. Through a case study, the researchers developed a welding control decision-making process designed to satisfy quality requirements and applied it to many welding conditions.

These studies demonstrate that DT can control machines and plants. DT-based control has the following advantages:

1. Commands the machines to optimize operations and enhance productivity.
2. Calculates methods to improve the production performance of numerous resources in the plant and command the resources.

#### 4.5.2 Case study

Park *et al*. (2020b) applied DT-based control to a micro smart factory (MSF) located in Daejeon, South Korea, to control the movement of facilities. The target manufacturing factory was a manufacturing system that produces prototypes for small and medium enterprises (Son *et al*., 2015). MSFs have processes for producing various products. These processes generally experience difficulty in responding to dynamic conditions, which affects the overall productivity.

To address this, a DT was applied that utilizes engineering applications and factory information collected at the site to respond to dynamic individualized production conditions for various products. As shown in Fig. 14 (Park *et al*., 2020b), this system architecture was designed to reflect the information collected at the actual site to the DT and link the DT with engineering applications to control the facilities in response to dynamic conditions. It comprises a total of four components to control the facilities through DT, which are described in detail as follows:

1. Manufacturing site: The actual MSF. Transmits the information of the actual manufacturing site plant to the DT manager via IIoT.
2. DT manager (service bus): Stores plant information collected in manufacturing. In addition, it sends grip point data on products, processes, plans, plants, resources, and machines to the engineering applications. After the engineering application is driven, the advanced planning and scheduling data (the result values) are sent to the DT manager and stored.
3. DT application: Provides monitoring functionality by synchronizing information on plant and manufacturing site conditions. In addition, it notifies the user when an abnormal situation occurs. Through engineering applications, the advanced planning and scheduling data representing the new solution are transmitted and reflected in the simulation to determine whether problems occur during the production period.
4. Engineering application: Consists of (i) an application that interacts with the MSF to determine whether abnormal situations and plan changes have occurred, (ii) an application that establishes product plans every day, (iii) an application that performs scheduling considering the production schedule, and (iv) an application that calculates the grip point of the machines. Using these, the advanced planning and scheduling and grip point data, which are needed to drive the DT, are transmitted to the DT manager.

## 5\. Discussion

### 5.1 Suggestions for future research through analysis of past and present research on the application of DT

When DT is utilized independently, it is only possible to implement prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, and (partly) improvement. In high-level DT functions, DT is interoperated with other engineering applications in the cases of improvement and control. That is, control and most of the improvement applications were difficult to realize when using the DT independently. Therefore, there is insufficient research on improvement and control of plant and logistics, which is a high level of DT functionality encompassing a wide target scope. Because the DT application must be integrated with at least one other engineering application, there are numerous factors to consider. Owing to the many required engineering applications, there are large quantities of data to collect and store and even more pre-processing procedures. Moreover, the data exchanged between them, the driving procedures, and the architecture or framework are complex.

More research is needed on high-level DT functionality with a wide target scope. In other words, studies are required on plant and logistics that cover a wide target scope within the improvement and control functions of the DT. Accordingly, to enable the use of high-level DT functions in a wide target scope, future studies are necessary on architectures or frameworks that can serve as references, driving procedures for entire systems, and defining the data required by engineering applications. Furthermore, in the studies in this paper, only one DT function was applied to a single application target at a time. However, future studies must simultaneously cover multiple functions of the DT applied to multiple application targets, as shown in Fig. 16. To realize smart manufacturing, DTs for design and manufacturing must be mutually integrated. The above-described schemes for utilizing DT functions will expand once the DTs for design and manufacturing are integrated.

### 5.2 System architecture for performing all functions of DT

Numerous studies applying DT for prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, improvement, and control have been conducted. However, when using only the DT application, it was only possible to implement prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, and part of improvement. To employ more DTs and perform advanced functions, they must be linked with other engineering applications according to their purpose.

This paper proposes a system architecture in which DT can perform prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, improvement, and control, and perform multiple functions instead of just one. The proposed system architecture comprises a total of four components, as shown in Fig. 17, which are described in detail as follows:

1. Manufacturing site: The actual manufacturing site. Transmits information on manufacturing resources to the legacy system and platform via IIoT.
2. Legacy system: It is a manufacturing system that stores information related to products and resources transmitted from the manufacturing site. This includes (i) material control system containing information related to the control phase of manufacturing resources, etc.; (ii) manufacturing execution system or point of production containing information related to the production operation phase, etc.; and (iii) enterprise resource planning (ERP) containing information about the company’s business phase, etc. It also processes and stores information sent from the manufacturing site and information about production orders and plans. Among the stored information, information necessary to operate manufacturing applications is aggregated and delivered to the cloud-based platform (DT manager).
3. Cloud-based platform (DT manager): Stores the information received from the legacy system, aggregates and pre-processes information for operating the manufacturing application, and delivers it to the manufacturing application. In addition, the result of DT application is visualized. The results of the DT application are converted into values that can be controlled by the manufacturing site and transmitted to the manufacturing site.
4. Manufacturing application:
	- Other engineering applications: These are engineering applications to improve the performance of DT’s functions. This is a scheduling and planning application to improve the performance of work planning, an AI application that predicts product defects or equipment downtime in advance, and an AR, MR, and VR application that can support user decision making by showing that the state of the manufacturing site, etc. are included. By using the information sent from the cloud-based platform, they are operated in connection with the DT application according to a set rule, and through this, the functions of DT can be improved.
		- DT application: A key component of this system architecture. DT configures, synchronizes, and executes information sent from cloud-based platforms and other engineering applications. Through this, DT performs five functions and delivers the result value to the cloud-based platform.

## 6\. Conclusion

This study analysed the current status of research on DT applied in the phases of the product lifecycle and in the hierarchy level axis of RAMI 4.0. It reviewed state-of-the-art studies on DT in terms of its industrial applications and core functions. According to the review, the DT functions were confirmed to be classified into prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, improvement, and control within the product lifecycle phases of design and manufacturing. This study also described representative cases and previous DT research using these functions within the application target scope of products, machines, processes, plants, and logistics, which comprise the hierarchy level axis of RAMI 4.0. It was found that past and present studies were performed on only a single function and application target scope. Moreover, there is insufficient research on high-level DT functions and application targets; the reasons for this were explained and future directions for DT studies were proposed. In terms of future DT research, a system architecture that can perform all functions was presented.

Almost 2 decades have passed since Grieves first proposed DT in 2003. Initial studies on DT focused on defining the concept. Over the last 5 years, however, research has made great strides in applying DT functions to industrial fields. Based on an analysis of DT research trends, the core functions that DT can perform are defined as follows:

1. Prototyping: In the design phase of new products, a DT is used to pre-verify the shape of the product and virtually check for problems that may occur after starting production or during use. The DT is also used to obtain data using a virtual product when the data are difficult to collect otherwise.
2. Pilot test: The DT is used to virtually confirm productivity and investment costs before adding or changing resource functions or locations in the manufacturing site. In addition, the DT can calculate the optimal manufacturing site layout using this procedure.
3. Monitoring: The DT is used to virtually check the status of machines (a resource in the manufacturing site) or the locations of products through real-time data synchronization.
4. Improvement: The DT is used to virtually calculate plans to enhance productivity-related performance at the manufacturing site to support decision making.
5. Control: After the DT performs improvement functions, it issues commands to the manufacturing site resources to improve productivity-related performance.

DT can perform a total of five functions: prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, improvement, and control. Based on this, DT can be defined as follows:

> “A virtual model that can perform prototyping, pilot testing, monitoring, improvement, and control to solve problems and situations in a manufacturing site based on information on the manufacturing site.”

Thus far, DT application studies have covered only a single function and application target scope. However, DT is still inadequate for replicating all parts of a manufacturing site. As such, further research is necessary on high-level DT functions in a wide application target scope. Moreover, DT must be applied to multiple functions and application targets, rather than a single function and target. Thus, by integrating DTs for design and manufacturing, DT will become a vital function in realizing smart manufacturing. For DT research to achieve this goal, it is necessary to define information models and system architectures of DT that can be applied to multiple functions and application targets. Research about how new ICTs, such as 5G networks, Edge and Fog Computing, and AR/MR/VR can be utilized and applied to DT is necessary, in order to raise the level of DT or commercialize it in actual manufacturing sites. To this end, cooperation among researchers from various fields is necessary.
