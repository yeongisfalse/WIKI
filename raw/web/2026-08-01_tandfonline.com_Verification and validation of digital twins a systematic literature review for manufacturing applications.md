# Verification and validation of digital twins: a systematic literature review for manufacturing applications

## ABSTRACT

Digital Twin (DT) is a concept of growing interest, driven by the technological advancements related to Industry 4.0. DT combines innovative technologies to create a virtual model replicating the physical system and allowing bi-directional data flow. However, to use DTs in support of decision-making, it is essential to have trust in the model and all components of the DT throughout its development. Verification and Validation (V&V) have traditionally been employed to assess models’ credibility, providing a venue for the development of trust. Therefore, V&V are essential foundations for developing DTs that can support decisions in real-world environments. Through a systematic literature review, this research investigated whether and how researchers are employing V&V in DTs for manufacturing applications. It was concluded that very little research was reported to have performed both verification and validation of the developed DTs. The study also examined the most commonly used V&V techniques and explored their relationship with DT capability level and application areas. It was concluded that there is a lack of standard procedures to conduct V&V and a lack of agreement on the V&V objectives. This research uncovers the main challenges to verifying and validating DTs and future research directions to develop trusted DTs.

## 1\. Introduction

The fourth industrial revolution, also known as Industry 4.0, relies on the implementation of digital technologies to gather and analyse real-time data, providing information to aid in decision-making for manufacturing systems, as well as immersive technologies that promote human-machine interaction (Eswaran et al. [Citation2024](#); Frank, Dalenogare, and Ayala [Citation2019](#); Inkulu et al. [Citation2022](#)). With the advancements of Industry 4.0 and related technologies, Digital Twin (DT) is a subject that is gaining interest in both industry and academia (Jones et al. [Citation2020](#)).

Since DT was conceived, there has been much debate about the definition and characteristics of a DT (Jones et al. [Citation2020](#); L. Zhang, Zhou, and Horn [Citation2021](#)). In 2003, Michael Grieves was the first to introduce the concept of a DT, describing it as a living system consisting of three main components: the physical product in real space, the virtual product in virtual space, and the bi-directional information and data flows connecting the physical system to its virtual counterpart (Grieves [Citation2014](#)). In a literature review, Kritzinger et al. ([Citation2018](#)) classified a DT into three subcategories according to the model’s level of integration: Digital Model, Digital Shadow, and Digital Twin. The authors described DT as a model where the digital counterpart is fully integrated with an existing physical system, creating a bidirectional and constant data flow between the physical object and the digital replica. However, definitions that describe a DT simply as a faithful representation of the physical system are not uncommon (Barricelli, Casiraghi, and Fogli [Citation2019](#)).

Besides the lack of a universal definition, it is fundamental to trust the data, accuracy, and updating procedure of the models that are supporting decision-making, especially in manufacturing systems where cost and safety are critical (Wright and Davidson [Citation2020](#)). As DTs gain prominence amid the Industry 4.0 landscape, the question that arises is how can we create DTs that can be trusted and used in real-world environments to support decision-making?

Verification and Validation (V&V) are crucial pillars of creating trust and credibility in DTs (Shao, Hightower, and Schindel [Citation2023](#); Wright and Davidson [Citation2020](#)). In a literature review, Tao et al. ([Citation2019](#)) mention the importance of V&V in the context of a DT. The authors stated that verification, validation, and accreditation procedures can validate the veracity of the model, providing the desired confidence level through the observation of the model error, algorithm error, and hardware error. Highlighting the importance of V&V procedures, Fuller et al. ([Citation2020](#)) purport that model V&V is the solution to overcome the challenges in developing trusted DTs, especially from an organisational point of view.

Although V&V concepts are well-developed and widely applied in many fields, such as modelling & simulation (Sargent [Citation2010](#)) and systems engineering (Walden et al. [Citation2015](#)), there is very little mention of V&V procedures in the context of DTs. Analysing the issue of V&V for DTs, Hua, Lazarova-Molnar, and Francis ([Citation2022](#)) concluded that there is no established method or structured framework for validating DTs. In addition, the authors pointed out that the absence of a structured approach toward DT validation may result in inconsistent levels of fidelity and robustness, ultimately affecting the overall effectiveness of DTs. Shao, Hightower, and Schindel ([Citation2023](#)) also identified a lack of a systematic approach to assessing the credibility of a DT. The authors suggest that the evolution of V&V techniques will become a key component for DT adoption, acceptance, and use at a greater scale.

This research aims to investigate the current state of V&V for DTs as the first step to developing a structured methodology to create DTs that can be trusted to be used in real-world environments. Furthermore, this work explores the current V&V procedures in the literature, highlighting the challenges of verifying and validating DTs in manufacturing applications. Finally, this work also sets future research directions for those who aim to contribute to this area of knowledge. Therefore, the main objectives of this research are:

- To investigate the current research and applications of V&V practices in DT through a Systematic Literature Review (SLR), focusing on applications in manufacturing.
- To discuss the current V&V procedures in the literature and challenges related to verifying and validating DTs.
- To set future research directions for the development of trusted DTs.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows: Section 2 provides a background of the related research, Section 3 reviews the SLR methodology, Section 4 presents the results and discusses the SLR findings, Section 5 details the challenges of V&V for DTs and future research directions, and Section 6 presents this work’s conclusions.

## 2\. Background

### 2.1. Digital twin

DTs offer unique insight into cyber-physical systems and provide important decision-making capabilities for the challenges manufacturers are currently facing in designing, building, and operating complex production systems by understanding how change impacts the process (Landy [Citation2023](#)). When Michael Grieves introduced the concept in 2003 (Grieves [Citation2014](#); Jones et al. [Citation2020](#); M. Liu et al. [Citation2021](#)), he described a DT as a model consisting of a physical product in the real space, a virtual product in the virtual space, and the bi-directional flow of data and information linking the physical system to its virtual counterpart (Grieves [Citation2014](#)).

Since the introduction of the DT concept, DTs have been applied in different fields and use cases leading to diverse definitions (Jones et al. [Citation2020](#); L. Zhang, Zhou, and Horn [Citation2021](#)). For instance, in the aerospace domain, Glaessgen and Stargel ([Citation2012](#)) and Tao et al. ([Citation2018](#)) defined a DT as an integrated multi-physics, multi-scale, probabilistic simulation of a system that uses data collected from sensors, historical data, physics models, etc. to replicate its correspondent physical twin. Focusing on applications in the manufacturing domain, Negri, Fumagalli, and Macchi ([Citation2017](#)) defined a DT as a virtual counterpart of a physical device that supports the design phase when used as a static model but also has the capability to update continuously, using sensed data, to allow real-time status and optimisation. In a literature review, Kritzinger et al. ([Citation2018](#)) state that although the terms Digital Model, Digital Replica, and Digital Twin were being used interchangeably to refer to the DT concept, a DT would require an existing physical object fully connected through data to a digital object. On the other hand, Grieves ([Citation2023](#)) argues that a twin can exist even without its physical counterpart in the form of a DT prototype during the design phase.

To standardise the concepts permeating DT technology, the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) proposed the ISO 23247:2021 standard to guide the development of DTs. The standard describes a DT as a fit-for-purpose digital representation of an observable manufacturing element (OME) with synchronisation between the element and its digital representation. OMEs can include personnel, equipment, materials, manufacturing processes, facilities, environment, and products in a manufacturing environment (International Organization for Standardization [Citation2021](#)).

In the absence of a consensus on the definition of a DT, Osho et al. ([Citation2022](#)) and Hyre et al. ([Citation2022](#)) described DTs in a maturity model based on their level of capability and complexity, called the 4R framework. This framework is composed of four phases: Representation, Replication, Reality, and Relational. In the Representation phase, the key features and behaviours for representing the physical system in a virtual space are identified. In accordance with these key features and relationships, data is collected from the physical system, stored, processed, and analysed. The Replication phase involves creating an accurate digital replica of the physical system using the data and connections developed during Representation. Then, in the Reality phase, beyond merely replicating a system, the DT can run independently and has the capability of predicting the physical system’s behaviour as well as providing what-if scenarios. Finally, in the Relational phase, the DT can solve problems powered by artificial intelligence. The Relational DT grants a degree of system autonomy, establishing a bidirectional data and information flow. A description for each phase is shown in Figure 1.

To differentiate models from DTs, Wright and Davidson ([Citation2020](#)) described a model as part of a DT that can offer a snapshot of a system's behaviour at a particular point in time. According to the authors, a model representing a physical system must have a dataset that connects the model to its corresponding physical environment and a data-driven updating process to become a DT. Therefore, although all DTs are models, not all models qualify as DTs. A DT lacking a virtual space, a corresponding physical or potential physical system, and a mechanism for updating through data exchange is simply a model. In addition to the definitions proposed by Grieves ([Citation2014](#)), a DT can have different capabilities and complexity levels depending on the system and the problem to be modelled. At its optimal level, any information that can be gathered by inspecting the physical system should be produced by its DT (Grieves [Citation2022](#)). As capability increases, DTs can provide an environment to support decision-making, predictions, and self-adjustment (Hyre et al. [Citation2022](#); Osho et al. [Citation2022](#)).

### 2.2. Trusted digital twins

Trust has been largely studied in engineering and management sciences as the foundation for decision-making in many situations and the driving force behind establishing relationships based on cooperation and collaboration (Cho, Chan, and Adali [Citation2015](#)). Although trust can be contemplated as a dynamic construct resulting from a continuous belief formation, model trust (or model credibility) is traditionally viewed from the perspective of V&V (Yilmaz and Liu [Citation2022](#)). In this context, establishing trust includes performing verification and validation activities to demonstrate how the V&V evidence can be applied to support the use of the model in a defined context of use. Therefore, trust is built by assessing the credibility of the model, which is obtained through the collection of evidence in the model’s capability to address a question of interest (The American Society of Mechanical Engineers [Citation2018](#)).

Similarly for DTs, performing V&V activities is fundamental to establishing the model’s credibility by ensuring that the DT meets its intended purpose and design goals (Shao, Hightower, and Schindel [Citation2023](#)). Furthermore, from an investment perspective, building trust in the DT implementation minimises the risk associated with technology adoption and acceptance (Bahmanziari, Pearson, and Crosby [Citation2003](#)). Although V&V is critical to achieving trusted DTs (Fuller et al. [Citation2020](#); Tao et al. [Citation2019](#); Wright and Davidson [Citation2020](#)), few works explored this aspect of DT development, leading to a lack of systematic treatment of V&V activities in DT models (Hua, Lazarova-Molnar, and Francis [Citation2022](#)).

### 2.3. V&V of models

Authorities from different fields have worked to define V&V processes and objectives. For instance, Oberkampf, Trucano, and Hirsch ([Citation2004](#)) define verification as the process of ensuring that a model implementation accurately represents the developer's conceptual description of the model and its solution. In simulation models, Sargent ([Citation2010](#)) defines verification as the process of ensuring that the computer programme and its implementation are correct. From a system and software perspective, verification is defined as the process of determining if the requirements for a system are completed and correct (International Organization for Standardization [Citation2017](#)). Therefore, verification aims to ensure that requirements are accurately met when transforming inputs into outputs (Walden et al. [Citation2015](#)). During verification, errors in the conceptual model are identified and quantified. However, verification does not determine whether the conceptual model does or does not represent the real world (Oberkampf, Trucano, and Hirsch [Citation2004](#)).

Validation, on the other hand, is the process of evaluating the accuracy of a model in representing the real world, from the perspective of its intended use. It provides evidence of how well the computational model simulates real-world system responses of interest (Oberkampf, Trucano, and Hirsch [Citation2004](#); Sargent [Citation2010](#)). From a systems and software perspective, validation is defined as the process of providing evidence that the system satisfies the intended use and user needs (International Organization for Standardization [Citation2017](#)). Therefore, the purpose of validation is to guarantee the system fulfils the stakeholder's needs by achieving its intended use in its intended operational environment (Walden et al. [Citation2015](#)).

Even though different fields have slightly different definitions when it comes to V&V, they all agree that verification and validation are different processes with different objectives. Both verification and validation activities need to be conducted to establish model credibility (The American Society of Mechanical Engineers [Citation2018](#)).

## 3\. Systematic literature review

Considering the discussed literature and the 4R framework proposed by Osho et al. ([Citation2022](#)) to characterise the levels of complexity and capability of DTs, a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) was conducted to assess the current state of the art concerning V&V applications for DTs in manufacturing. This SLR is an extension and more comprehensive review of the study first presented in Bitencourt et al. ([Citation2023](#)).

The methodology of the SLR developed in this research is summarised in Figure 2. For investigating the current state of the art concerning V&V methods for DT, two main Research Questions (RQ) were formulated:

- RQ1: Does the research literature incorporate V&V to DT applications in manufacturing?
- RQ2: Is the difference between verification and validation clear for Digital Twin applications in manufacturing?

Figure 2. SLR methodology.

[![Figure illustrating the systematic literature review methodology, including research questions, and the steps to locate, select, and review articles.](https://www.tandfonline.com/cms/asset/f27757a0-687a-464c-9d0d-c4e788e76c4b/tprs_a_2357741_f0002_oc.jpg)](#)

Answering the proposed RQs will provide an overview of V&V for DT in manufacturing, as well as establish if the current methodologies support the development of trusted DT models. As observed in Section 2.2, there is a reported lack of V&V for DTs. Therefore, RQ1 aims to provide a detailed review of V&V for DT in manufacturing, including the most common areas of application, types of V&V techniques employed by authors, and insights regarding V&V at different DT maturity levels and application areas. RQ2 aims to investigate if the differentiation between the verification and validation processes is clear in the current literature when developing DTs. RQ2 is particularly important because, as presented in Section 2.3, verification and validation are distinct processes with unique objectives. Therefore, to develop DTs that can be trusted, it is essential to perform both verification and validation activities. RQ2 will establish if the V&V activities that are currently employed can support the development of trusted DTs.

The search engines Science Direct, Engineering Village, and Web of Science were used because they offered comprehensive coverage of the literature relevant to the domain and provided advanced search options that enabled control over search parameters. Based on the research questions, a combination of search terms was identified by testing and iteratively refining the search parameters. The articles had to contain ‘digital twin’ in the title, ‘manufacturing OR production OR fabrication OR assembly’ in either the abstract, title, or keywords, and variations of the terms ‘verification’ and ‘validation’ in any part of the article. Only journal articles and conference papers in English were considered, resulting in a population of 315 papers. Next, duplicates were eliminated, resulting in 269 unique titles.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were established, and titles and abstracts were analysed. Only publications intended for creating and applying DTs in manufacturing environments were included, resulting in 188 publications for full-text review. A few articles were excluded during the full-text review because, although the abstract stated that an application would be presented, the paper lacked such an application.

The final step of the SLR was an in-depth full-text review consisting of two phases: classification of DT capabilities (Phase 1) and classification of V&V procedures (Phase 2). In Phase 1, the papers were categorised according to the level of DT achieved, observing the characteristics of each ‘R’ described in the 4R framework, and the DT’s capabilities reported by the authors. Furthermore, research articles claiming to be DTs that did not fit in any of the 4R levels were classified as ‘not DTs.’ This first phase is critical because it was observed from the literature that there is a lack of consensus in the definition of a DT (Jones et al. [Citation2020](#); L. Zhang, Zhou, and Horn [Citation2021](#)), but at the same time, minimum characteristics need to be present to differentiate a DT from other types of models, such as simulation models. Using a maturity model like the 4R framework allows for consideration of DTs in the multiple stages of development, providing the flexibility needed to accommodate different levels of abstraction according to the capabilities implemented by researchers (Osho et al. [Citation2022](#)).

With the DTs identified, Phase 2 consisted of categorising the papers according to the reported V&V procedure, i.e. if the DT was verified and validated according to the authors. Throughout this step, it was also observed if authors were using verification and validation as synonyms to describe the same process, which is referred to as interchangeability. Appendix A provides a detailed description of the evidence used to perform the 4R classification and identify the V&V status in the analysed articles.

### 4.1. Phase 1: identification of DTs using the 4R framework

In Phase 1 of the in-depth full-text review, the papers were classified regarding the capability level achieved according to the 4R framework. The classification in the 4R framework was fundamental to distinguish DT applications from other technologies while considering DT implementation maturity level. Hence, minimum data integration and synchronisation characteristics were considered in the classification process. Conventional offline modelling with historical data, or models that did not provide the capability of synchronising real data collected from the physical system to its corresponding virtual environment, were classified as ‘not DT’. Figure 4 summarises the results of Phase 1 of the SLR.

Figure 4. 4R framework classification results.

[![Pie chart showing the 4R framework classification results.](https://www.tandfonline.com/cms/asset/ce5bb0d4-6f6a-4193-bf2a-02f20602cf8f/tprs_a_2357741_f0004_oc.jpg)](#)

In Phase 1, 49% of the selected publications were classified as not DTs according to the definitions adopted by this research. Approximately 15% of the published research described DTs at the Representation level, detailing the building of a real-time data collection pipeline, as well as data storage, and analysis. Most of the publications (26%) that presented research on DTs were classified at the Replication level, in that they reported successfully using real data to create a virtual replica, and the virtual replica could reproduce the same outputs of the physical system. Only a few researchers (10%) were able to build a DT capable of replicating the physical system that could also predict the outcome of how the physical system will perform achieving the Reality level. None of the examined publications reached the Relational level exploring advanced problem-solving capabilities, autonomy, and self-calibrating driven by the DT.

For the identified DTs, an evolution of DT capability can be observed throughout the years (Figure 5). As the interest in DT applications increases, Reality level DTs become more prominent supported by the incorporation of multiple data sources (e.g. PLC and sensors), machine learning, and advanced simulation (e.g. game engines for real-time simulation of system’s physics), leading to a natural progression of DT capabilities.

[![A bar chart showing the number of articles for each 4R framework level over the years.](https://www.tandfonline.com/cms/asset/041d5d97-0091-4a82-9015-2eb24aa78e0e/tprs_a_2357741_f0005_oc.jpg)](#)

However, the results of the 4R classification corroborate the discussion about the lack of consensus on the DT definition, as many researchers are developing models that do not fall under any of the DT levels of the 4R framework. One common occurrence is traditional simulation models incorrectly classified as DTs. Although both simulation and DT rely on digital models to replicate the physical system, these models exhibit distinct individual characteristics. While traditional simulation uses a conventional offline approach to gather inputs, apply rules, and perform analysis of multiple what-if scenarios, a DT promotes the use of a bidirectional data flow and information in synchronisation with the physical system. Although both can be used in combination, it is important to distinguish between the characteristics of the technologies, as the need for each approach will depend on the problem to be solved and the capability and complexity levels one aims to achieve when modelling the physical system. For instance, as examples of models that did not provide such data integration or synchronisation, Discrete-Event Simulation (DES) was often used to implement the proposed DT of the physical system (Abdoune et al. [Citation2023](#); Denkena et al. [Citation2020](#); Frantzén, Bandaru, and Ng [Citation2022](#); Ghosh et al. [Citation2021](#); Lugaresi et al. [Citation2023](#); Neto et al. [Citation2023](#); Sommer et al. [Citation2023](#); Tissot et al. [Citation2022](#); Z. Zhang, Yeming, and Zailin [Citation2022](#)). Simulation models were also often applied to the conceptual phase of a system or product, in which the proposed DT is utilised in the place of a physical prototype or to predict the future system behaviour (Ayani, Ganebäck, and Ng [Citation2018](#); Bilberg and Malik [Citation2019](#); Cimino et al. [Citation2023](#); Fernandes, Crhistian, and Donatelli [Citation2021](#); Gross and Svasta [Citation2023](#); Lee et al. [Citation2022](#); Malik and Brem [Citation2021](#); Mourtzis, Angelopoulos, and Panopoulos [Citation2021](#); Vinodh Kumar et al. [Citation2023](#); Xiao, Qi, and Tao [Citation2023](#); Wei et al. [Citation2023](#)). Another common application was to predict the mechanical properties of materials and products, such as fatigue, crack initiation, and solidification outcomes (Fisher, Nygren, and Beaudoin [Citation2022](#); Guivarch et al. [Citation2019](#); Kim et al. [Citation2022](#); Knapp et al. [Citation2017](#); Ye et al. [Citation2020](#); H. Zhang et al. [Citation2023](#)). Moreover, DTs with simulation-like capabilities were used for layout design (Mo et al. [Citation2023](#)), to augment data sets and to generate training data (Z. Zhang et al. [Citation2023](#)), identification of assembly defects (Pang et al. [Citation2023](#)), support predictive maintenance (Abbate et al. [Citation2022](#); Mi et al. [Citation2021](#); Neto et al. [Citation2021](#); Qiao et al. [Citation2019](#); Yan, Wang, and Wu [Citation2022](#)), and to address scheduling problems (Fang et al. [Citation2019](#); Lin et al. [Citation2021](#); Tissot et al. [Citation2022](#)). DTs were also commonly associated with digital models, such as Computer-Aided Design (CAD), used in combination with Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM) and MATLAB to simulate the physical system (Anbalagan, Shivakrishna, and Srikanth [Citation2021](#); Hinchy et al. [Citation2020](#); Rosado, Aristimuño, and Arrazola [Citation2023](#); Vasileiou et al. [Citation2021](#); Xi et al. [Citation2021](#)).

Additionally, DTs are being developed to a certain extent, but not to their full potential as none of the analysed DTs reached the Relational level, i.e. applications with high-level decision-making capabilities and autonomy. Some of the reasons why DTs are not achieving higher levels of capability could be the disconnection between the DT concept and application, the challenges related to technology implementation and data acquisition in real-world scenarios, and the limited case studies that can be replicated as implementation details are often omitted (Wooley, Silva, and Bitencourt [Citation2023](#)).

The results of this analysis also strengthen the need for a common definition of DTs. The absence of a standard definition leads to inconsistency in the applications and can hinder the development and advancement of new capabilities. Maturity models, such as the 4R framework, can help bridge the gap between the definition first proposed by Grieves and real-world applications.

### 4.2. Phase 2: V&V of DTs in manufacturing applications

Once the DT applications were identified, Phase 2 of the analysis could be performed to answer RQ1 and RQ2. Therefore, the most common areas of application in manufacturing, as well as an overview of common V&V techniques and methods could be investigated. Furthermore, this research also explored the V&V evolution throughout the years, the relationship between V&V and DT maturity levels, and whether authors were able to distinguish between verification and validation activities, as both processes are fundamental to the development of trusted DTs.

#### 4.2.1. DT areas of application

In an overview of the application areas of the identified DTs, most of the articles were developed in generic manufacturing environments (47%), usually composed of multiple subtractive or additive machines and robots in a production line format. Most applications in this category were also deployed in laboratory environments, which explains the generic nature of the applications as researchers aim to create methods that apply to a variety of scenarios. The manufacturing of metal parts is the second most common area of application (10%), followed by the production of electrical equipment, appliances, and components (7%). Applications were also observed in the transportation industry, with DTs being used in the fabrication of automotive vehicles, aircraft, and ships (6%), as well as in aerospace (5%) and additive manufacturing operations (4%). #### 4.2.2. V&V overview and common techniques

When it comes to the V&V applied to DTs, the selected articles underwent an in-depth full-text review, in which it was observed whether or not authors reported V&V activities for the developed DTs. Figure 7 summarises the results for the V&V classification for all DTs analysed and for each capability level according to the 4R framework.

As can be observed in Figure 7(a), most of the analysed studies claimed at least some form of verification or validation. However, few researchers reported both verifying and validating the developed DTs (24%). As illustrated in Figure 7(b), DTs developed to the Reality level reported the highest proportion of models that have been both verified and validated while also reporting no articles in which V&V activities were not mentioned. This fact may indicate that, as DT models become more complex, V&V activities become even more crucial for DT credibility.

For models that have been both verified and validated (24%), different techniques were employed by researchers. Nie et al. ([Citation2023](#)) developed verification through a case study to demonstrate the effectiveness of the adopted DT and validation as the process of comparing the adopted solutions with a previously developed algorithm, showing that the errors between DT predicted values and physical system were smaller throughout the entire validation process. Some of the indicators used to measure DT accuracy were mean absolute error, mean square error, and mean absolute percentage error. J. Lv et al. ([Citation2023](#)) performed the verification process by observing the model's accuracy based on target scenarios, corresponding to new spindle speed, feed rate, and sensor axis. Moreover, the authors conducted validation by developing the DT in a realistic environment in a case study. Chen et al. ([Citation2023](#)) also performed both verification and validation of the developed DT. In the verification process, the authors experimentally observed the correctness of the temperature prediction and tool wear values using thermal imaging cameras and different tool wear levels. The results were compared to traditional cutting parameters to demonstrate the increase in performance by implementing the DT. In the validation process, the authors performed cross-validation to prevent overfit and observe algorithm accuracy using root mean square error as an indicator. Mykoniatis and Harris ([Citation2021](#)) performed verification by conducting visual inspection against video recordings, simulation animation, and input/output state diagrams. Verification also included the process of testing DT responses to evaluate the correct workflow and total processing time at each station. For validation, on the other hand, the authors performed statistical testing with selected key performance indicators, comparing the DT with the actual production system.

Additionally, Z. Wang, Ouyang, and Kochan ([Citation2023](#)) conducted the verification process by comparing real-time data with historical data and calculating the mean absolute percentage error between the replicated state and the actual operating state. The validation process, however, refers to the case study in which a game engine was used to create the DT and to demonstrate its real-time capability using average time intervals. Osho et al. ([Citation2022](#)) proposed a DT for a fused deposition modelling additive manufacturing machine, in which the verification process is mostly focused on data accuracy. Therefore, the data captured from the sensors installed in the 3D printer were compared with other verification sources. Extruder heating and cooling temperature readings were also evaluated under different environmental factors. Although validation is also used to refer to the process of accuracy measurement, the authors mentioned the case study performed in the 3D printer to validate the proposed DT framework’s usability. J. Wang et al. ([Citation2022](#)) also developed a case study to validate the DT of an energy power battery. In the verification process, the authors performed DT testing to guarantee the mapping between the physical and virtual models. In a petrochemical industry application, Min et al. ([Citation2019](#)) accomplished validation of the DT algorithm using a validation dataset, observing indicators such as error of fitting, model accuracy ratio, root mean square error, variance interpretation rate, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The validation process also included the process of comparing the practical effect of the DT model with traditional production control methods. For verification, on the other hand, the authors refer to the process of testing the DT in a real-time production environment to verify its effectiveness and security as the last step before actually deploying the model into production control practices. Finally, Q. Wang, Jiao, and Zhang ([Citation2020](#)) performed verification through an application case study to demonstrate the effectiveness of the developed deep learning-based method that is integrated into the DT, and validation using validation datasets for the machine learning model component.

Model-based system engineering approaches were also used to perform V&V. For instance, J. Liu et al. ([Citation2021](#)) used the Vee model to guide the development of a shop-floor DT, performing verification against the requirements for DT synchronisation, and validation against stakeholder needs, evaluating model completeness. Also using a model-based system engineering approach, X. Zhang et al. ([Citation2023](#)) performed a three-level verification process. The first verification step investigated the synchronisation of the DT to guarantee that the digital replica would accurately represent the progress status of the physical system. The authors also used the DT 3D representation to check the appearance, functions, and performance of the DT. Finally, the authors also compared virtual sensor data with actual sensor data to verify the predicted status. As for validation activities, the authors used a requirement tracking matrix to map stakeholder requirements with functions and elements, making sure that the architecture developed met the established requirement while keeping traceability. Park et al. ([Citation2023](#)) verified the DT requirements based on three perspectives: verification of the asset description, verification of the operating procedures, and verification of the service composition. Validation, on the other hand was performed in two designed scenarios, observing the accuracy of the DT-based model and the DT action time. (Fan et al. [Citation2021](#)) performed verification based on user requirements determined in the project planning stage, and validation through a case study developed in an automotive facility.

For articles that reported to have only verified the developed DTs (31%), authors frequently mentioned case studies as a technique for verification (Eunike et al. [Citation2022](#); Jia, Wang, and Zhang [Citation2023](#); Kumbhar, Ng, and Bandaru [Citation2023](#); Leng et al. [Citation2020](#); [Citation2022](#); Liang et al. [Citation2022](#); J. Liu et al. [Citation2022](#); Y. Liu et al. [Citation2022](#); Sun et al. [Citation2020](#); Y. Yang et al. [Citation2023](#); G. Wang et al. [Citation2021](#); J. Wang et al. [Citation2023](#); Y. Wang et al. [Citation2022](#); Wei et al. [Citation2021](#); [Citation2022](#); Q. Zhang et al. [Citation2022](#)). In this context, comparisons considering the system performance before and after the DT implementation, different operations conditions, the performance of alternative methods, and the agreement between physical and virtual systems are often used to demonstrate the DT operational capability. For instance, Leng et al. ([Citation2020](#)) compared the existing smartphone assembly line to the one designed and managed by the proposed DT approach observing various factors, such as makespan, line balance, and equipment utilisation. Y. Yang et al. ([Citation2023](#)) performed verification experiments of a rescheduling DT under two conditions, several single-order arrivals and a single arrival of multiple orders, observing the system reaction time, makespan, and energy reduction. J. Wang et al. ([Citation2023](#)) used a simulation of an industrial enterprise to verify the effectiveness of the DT by comparing the performance of the proposed approach with two other methodologies for static and dynamic environments. Q. Zhang et al. ([Citation2022](#)) provided an implementation of a DT of a drill-rivet machine to verify the practicability of the DT. The case study compared the developed DT with a traditional assembly process using nine performance indicators. Lastly, Wei et al. ([Citation2021](#)) investigated the DT consistency by comparing the average deviation between the physical and DT in a sequence of tests.

For articles that reported to have only validated the developed DTs (35%), case studies were also often associated with testing the DT in a realistic environment to demonstrate operational capability (Alam, Kabir, and Mirmohammadsadeghi [Citation2023](#); Dobaj et al. [Citation2022](#); Kherbache, Maimour, and Rondeau [Citation2022](#); Latsou, Farsi, and Erkoyuncu [Citation2023](#); Leng et al. [Citation2021](#); C. Liu, Jiang, and Jiang [Citation2020](#); S. Liu, Wang, and Wang [Citation2022](#); X. Liu et al. [Citation2022](#); Longo, Nicoletti, and Padovano [Citation2019](#); Modoni, Stampone, and Trotta [Citation2022](#); Morabito et al. [Citation2023](#); Zheng and Sivabalan [Citation2020](#); Zhu et al. [Citation2021](#)). Comparisons were also common to demonstrate the performance of the DT. For instance, Latsou, Farsi, and Erkoyuncu ([Citation2023](#)) validated the DT against data collected from the studied warehouse, comparing the actual cycle times with the DT cycle times for four manufacturing activities. The authors demonstrated agreement between virtual and physical environments under normal and abnormal conditions. The DT could also detect anomalous values in input RFID data and realise their impacts on the system performance. Alam, Kabir, and Mirmohammadsadeghi ([Citation2023](#)) validated the DT by comparing the performance of the production line before and after DT implementation and observing the line output. Leng et al. ([Citation2021](#)) developed a case study comparing the traditional commissioning approach and the DT-based approach, observing the reduction of commissioning time and iterations. Modoni, Stampone, and Trotta ([Citation2022](#)) validated the correctness and capability of the proposed DT through two test sessions. In the first session, the DT was tested in a simulated scenario for multiple conditions. Then, the second session of testing was conducted in real process conditions. Visual inspection was also performed to validate the DT responses for non-compliant parts.

([Citation2023](#)) performed verification by demonstrating model calibration and executing field debugging to test the connection between the cyber-physical space and Perno, Hvam, and Haug ([Citation2023](#)) conducted validation using personal and group interviews with specialists and management to evaluate the data quality and DT model accuracy. #### 4.2.3. V&V status and the lack of a common objective

Through the analysis of techniques employed as well as the objectives of V&V activities developed by authors and detailed in Section 4.2.2, it can be observed that although V&V was performed at some level in most of the investigated articles, there is a clear lack of a standard procedure. This lack of standard procedure results in V&V activities that rely on strong assumptions, simplified scenarios, and average system behaviour, making the result evaluation challenging. Furthermore, it was also observed low traceability of the V&V techniques to the DT requirements and objectives, except for the few approaches that used model-based techniques (Fan et al. [Citation2021](#); J. Liu et al. [Citation2021](#); Park et al. [Citation2023](#); X. Zhang et al. [Citation2023](#)). The level of detail in the V&V process described also varied between studies, with some presenting very detailed approaches (J. Liu et al. [Citation2021](#)), and others providing little or no specifics of the procedures (H. Li et al. [Citation2022](#); Panagou et al. [Citation2022](#); Yavari et al. [Citation2021](#)).

The lack of standard procedure for V&V activities can be understood by its still growing maturity. As shown in Figure 9, the number of DTs that reported being both verified and validated has increased in recent years. The crescent interest in V&V activities could be also related to the natural progression of DT capabilities observed in Figure 5, led by the implementation of multiple data acquisition technologies, advanced simulation, and artificial intelligence.

When it comes to the areas of application, V&V activities are employed unevenly throughout manufacturing areas, suggesting that V&V activities develop at a different pace depending on the environment of the application. As illustrated in Figure 10, additive manufacturing has the highest number of works reported being both verified and validated (75%), followed by oil and gas (50%) and electrical, appliance, and component applications (40%).

Moreover, from the studies that reported being able to both verify and validate the developed DTs, 9 used at some point verification and validation interchangeably as synonyms to refer to the same process, which corresponds to almost half of the investigated models (47%) (Fan et al. [Citation2021](#); Guardabascio et al. [Citation2022](#); Hu et al. [Citation2023](#); J. Lv et al. [Citation2023](#); Nie et al. [Citation2023](#); Osho et al. [Citation2022](#); Ouadoudi, Schwarz, and Borcsok [Citation2022](#); J. Wang et al. [Citation2022](#); X. Yang et al. [Citation2023](#)). This interchangeability suggests that there is a lack of agreement over the objectives of V&V for DTs. This fact becomes even more evident through the observation of the techniques employed for V&V and presented in detail in Section 4.2.2. Most of the analysed publications use case studies to showcase the performance and accuracy of DT for both verification and validation, despite the distinct nature and objectives of these processes.

Thus far, the V&V classification has been based on the authors’ claims, as discussed in the SLR methodology. Therefore, the classification performed in Phase 2 of the SLR did not consider the V&V definitions presented in the literature. However, to provide a more comprehensive status of V&V activities, as well as if authors can distinguish the unique objectives of V&V, defining verification and validation in the context of DTs is necessary. Considering the concepts presented in Section 2.3, this research defined verification and validation for DTs as follows:

- Verification: the process of providing evidence that the DT has achieved its specified requirements and that the requirements are implemented correctly. All significant features and relationships defined during the requirements definition, implemented during the DT development, and tested during the evaluation stage in fact reproduce the conceptual model of the physical system.
- Validation: the process of providing evidence that the DT is an accurate representation of the physical system, considering a specific context of use. Context of use can be defined as the environment in which the DT will be deployed to solve a specific problem or research question. It is determined whether the DT satisfies its purpose and the desired operational capability in a realistic context.

Comparing the techniques and procedures implemented by the studies analysed in this SLR (Section 4.2.2.) to the definitions presented above reinforces the idea that there is no agreement over the difference between verification and validation procedures for DTs, as the majority of the investigated articles actually showcase validation, but not verification. Furthermore, the fact that verification and validation are commonly used as synonyms, even though they are different processes with different associated objectives is particularly concerning for real-world applications since V&V are pillars for building trust in models, and thus in DTs. It is imperative that both the verification and validation processes take place to develop a model that can be trusted and used in real-world environments.

Some of the reasons for this lack of agreement over the V&V procedure can be related to the unique characteristics of DTs. Since DTs incorporate various Industry 4.0 technologies (e.g. Internet-of-Things, machine learning, and simulation), the development of such cyber-physical environments is not trivial and therefore requires a structured approach to thoroughly verify and validate the interactions among these components. A clear definition of the V&V objectives in the context of DTs is needed, as well as a structured approach to guide developers to verify and validate their DTs.

## 5\. Challenges of V&V in DTs

Although most of the applications of DT found in the analysis claimed to have used at least some form of verification or validation, few reported both verifying and validating the developed DTs. This study reveals that there is still a disconnect between the theory and practice of V&V for DTs. Based on this research findings, the main challenges to implementing V&V in DTs and future research directions were identified.

### 5.1. DT unique characteristics and complexity

The existing V&V approaches are not specifically designed to address the unique characteristics and the dynamic nature of DTs. As DT combines various technologies and methods to provide an environment capable of representing the physical system, it is fundamental that the V&V techniques also reflect this integration of multiple technologies. Furthermore, the complexity of DTs is evident in their multifaceted structure, comprised of numerous interconnected components, data sources, and real-time synchronisation. Each of these components contributes to the overall performance and reliability of the DT. Therefore, it is essential to have a methodology that can verify and validate all the pieces that compose a DT.

Future research directions on integrating best practices in V&V from multiple fields are needed to create a methodology that incorporates the multiple facets of DT. Credibility assessment frameworks could be adapted from other fields, such as computer modelling, to address DT's unique characteristics throughout its lifecycle (Shao, Hightower, and Schindel [Citation2023](#)). Moreover, the incorporation of simulation best practices and model-based systems engineering are promising alternatives for prioritising V&V activities.

### 5.2. Lack of a standardised methodology

As observed in the SLR and pointed out by earlier studies (Hua, Lazarova-Molnar, and Francis [Citation2022](#); Shao, Hightower, and Schindel [Citation2023](#)), there is an absence of an accepted standardised methodology to conduct V&V activities for DTs. This lack of a structured approach leads DT developers to rely on strong assumptions and simplified scenarios. The V&V process also often lacks implementation details, mainly focusing on demonstrating operational capability. Few articles provide quantifiable requirements that can be traceable to the V&V method employed, leaving V&V results open to subjectivity and dependent on the average system behaviour. Although the application of V&V activities have grown through the years, progress still needs to be made toward a standardised procedure.

As V&V becomes a fundamental component of DT adoption and acceptance, future research directions could be concentrated not only on incorporating best practices from multiple fields but also on providing a standardised and structured approach for conducting V&V. Model-based approaches that emphasise the need to establish requirements are a promising solution for its versatility. Case studies demonstrating the framework in multiple environments could also help increase adoption and decrease the uneven V&V implementation throughout the different areas of application, as also observed in the SLR.

### 5.3. Lack of agreement in V&V objectives

As shown by this research, there is a lack of agreement over the objectives of verification and validation activities for DTs in manufacturing. Therefore, many of the examined studies focused only on demonstrating the operational capability of the DT, with minimal attention given to what is needed to achieve the designed capability and the rationale behind the selected solutions. This can explain the reason most of the reported V&V efforts are in validation instead of verification. This lack of understanding of the different objectives of verification and validation can jeopardise the effective application of appropriate methods to ensure the credibility of DTs. Therefore, to establish trust in DTs, it is essential to perform both verification and validation, contrary to the prevalent trends observed in this study.

Future research directions could be related to the development of case studies and applications focusing not only on validation but also on verification activities. Such applications could serve as a guideline for developers who aim to create trusted DTs that can support decision-making.

### 5.4. Metrics to determine V&V capability in DTs

Without metrics to analyse the capability and capacity of a DT when compared to the associated physical system the hope of a consistent and accepted definition of verification and validation will not come to pass. Developing metrics that drive consistency in the development and use of DTs will provide users with an understanding of the capability of the DT and identify those models that some are calling DTs as lacking in the level of robustness needed for the application. Metrics could help refine the development process to ensure that the DTs being developed truly satisfy the desired requirements.

### 5.5. Measure of trust

The concept of trust is still a very subjective topic in the development and use of DTs. There is a need for the development of a true value for trust in the DT domain in a manufacturing environment. Without an objective measure of trust, the rate of adoption of this new technology will continue to be a subject of research and little application in the manufacturing realm. Researchers should investigate the concept of trust development from other domains and create a framework for an objective measure of trust in DTs.

## 6\. Conclusion

In this research, the current state of V&V for DT applications in manufacturing was investigated through an SLR. It was concluded that very few works were reported to have verified and validated the developed DTs. Furthermore, there is a lack of agreement and understanding over the V&V objectives, as well as a standard procedure to conduct V&V activities. Challenges and future research opportunities were identified to set the future course of V&V of trusted DTs.

The limitations of this study are related to the databases included in the SLR. Although it is believed that the presented search engines offer a comprehensive overview of the current state-of-the-art, it should be noted that this study is limited to articles found on the mentioned databases after iteratively refining the search parameters.

The findings of this research lead to several future research opportunities. First, a common definition for DTs needs to be developed. Maturity models, such as the 4R framework used in this study can help accommodate the different levels of capability a DT can have depending on the context of use and application. Second, a structured method for conducting V&V should be explored by researchers to appropriately verify and validate DTs, as well as to develop DTs that can be trusted to be used in real-world environments. Case studies that demonstrate the structured approach are also necessary to give clear guidelines to developers on how to build trusted DTs through V&V. Furthermore, V&V techniques could also be further explored in future research, promoting the need to perform both verification and validation to achieve trusted DTs. Finally, traceable and quantifiable methods to achieve trusted DTs are also fundamental. Future research efforts could also focus on methods to reduce the subjectivity of V&V, considering the unique characteristics of DTs.
